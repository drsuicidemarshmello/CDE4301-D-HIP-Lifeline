## Introduction
This project is undertaken as part of the Duke-NUS Health Innovator Program (DHIP), which brings together individuals from different academic disciplines to identify unmet clinical needs and develop innovative healthcare solutions. Our multidisciplinary team comprises five fellows with complementary backgrounds: Abhay Menon and Phua Kai Jie (Clinical Fellows), Sharleen Hu and Kirby Lee (Engineering Fellows), and Michelle Tan (Business Fellow). By integrating clinical, engineering, and business perspectives, the team aims to develop solutions that are clinically relevant, technically feasible, and commercially viable.

The project is guided by our clinical mentors, Dr Too and Dr Heng, from the Department of Vascular and Interventional Radiology at Singapore General Hospital (SGH), alongside our industry mentor, Matt Durack, Director of Strategic Innovation at BD.

The project was initiated with the following problem statement provided by our clinical mentors: **A safe and reliable way to deliver long-term systemic chemotherapy for oncology patients while reducing vascular access-related complications, treatment interruptions, and patient burden. This initial problem statement serves as the foundation for our team's exploration of existing vascular access methods used in chemotherapy delivery.** Through clinical observations, stakeholder interviews, and research into current practices, our team seeks to identify key challenges and opportunities for innovation that could improve the safety, reliability, and overall patient experience of long-term chemotherapy administration.

## Clinical Discovery

### Why Cancer?
Cancer remains a major public health concern in Singapore, with the number of newly diagnosed cases increasing over the years. According to the Singapore Cancer Registry Annual Report 2023, a total of 91,574 new cancer cases were reported in Singapore between 2019 and 2023, equivalent to an average of approximately 50 new cancer diagnoses per day (National Registry of Diseases Office, 2026).

This growing cancer burden highlights the importance of improving cancer treatment and care delivery. Chemotherapy remains an important treatment modality for many cancer patients, often requiring multiple treatment cycles administered over an extended period. For the purpose of this project, long-term chemotherapy is defined by our clinical mentors as chemotherapy treatment lasting three months or longer. During this period, patients may undergo repeated chemotherapy cycles, each requiring reliable vascular access for drug administration.

As such, safe and reliable long-term venous access is essential to facilitate repeated chemotherapy administration while minimising vascular access-related complications, treatment interruptions, and patient discomfort. Improving existing vascular access solutions could therefore contribute to a safer and more comfortable treatment experience, ultimately enhancing the quality of life of patients undergoing long-term chemotherapy.

### Types of Vascular Access
There are three main types of vascular access devices commonly used for chemotherapy administration: peripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs), peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs), and totally implantable venous access ports (Port-a-Caths). Each method offers distinct advantages and limitations in terms of ease of insertion, infection risk, patient comfort, and suitability for long-term chemotherapy administration.

#### Peripheral Intravenous Catheter
A PIVC is a short, flexible cannula inserted into a peripheral vein, typically in the hand or forearm, to provide temporary vascular access for the administration of medications and fluids. One of its main advantages is that it can be placed by a trained healthcare professional without requiring a surgical procedure. They also generally carry a lower risk of infection. 

However, PIVC insertion can be challenging in patients with difficult-to-access veins, sometimes requiring multiple attempts to locate and cannulate a suitable vein. Repeated needle punctures can cause pain and discomfort, while frequent cannulation and exposure to certain chemotherapy drugs may lead to phlebitis (inflammation of the vein) and potential venous damage. Over time, repeated vascular injury may compromise vein integrity, making subsequent venous access increasingly difficult.

#### Peripherally Inserted Central Catheter
A PICC is a long, flexible catheter inserted through a peripheral vein, typically in the upper arm, and advanced until its tip reaches a central vein near the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium. PICC insertion requires a specialised procedure, typically performed under ultrasound guidance and X-ray verification by an interventional radiologist. One of the main advantages of a PICC is its versatility, as it can be used to administer various medications, including chemotherapy, antibiotics, and intravenous nutrition. Some PICCs also contain multiple lumens, allowing different compatible treatments to be administered through separate channels. Furthermore, the external catheter is equipped with a standard Luer-lock connection, enabling trained nursing staff to connect compatible infusion equipment directly without repeated needle punctures.

However, as a PICC has an external segment that protrudes from the skin, this creates a potential route for microorganisms to enter, increasing the risk of catheter-related infections. Another potential complication is catheter occlusion caused by fibrin sheath formation. A fibrin sheath is a thin layer of protein-rich material that develops around the catheter following its insertion into the bloodstream. In some cases, this sheath may extend over the catheter tip and act as a one-way valve, allowing fluid to be infused into the bloodstream while preventing blood from being aspirated back through the catheter. This can interfere with catheter functionality and may require additional intervention to restore normal operation.

#### Totally Implantable Venous Access Ports
A Port-a-Cath is a small, fully implanted device that provides long-term central venous access for the administration of chemotherapy and other medications. The device consists of a subcutaneous reservoir connected to a catheter, with its tip positioned in a central vein. To administer medication, a trained healthcare professional uses a specialised Huber needle to puncture the skin and access the port reservoir. The medication then flows through the connected catheter and is delivered directly into the central circulation. One of the main advantages of a Port-a-Cath is its lower risk of catheter-related infection compared to a PICC, as the device is fully implanted beneath the skin without an external catheter segment. Furthermore, once the port is implanted, patients generally experience less discomfort during repeated access compared to PIVC, making it particularly suitable for long-term chemotherapy administration.

However, Port-a-Cath placement requires a more invasive insertion procedure, involving the creation of a subcutaneous pocket to accommodate the port reservoir and the placement of a catheter into the central venous system. Additionally, unlike a PICC, which can be accessed through a standard Luer-lock connection, a Port-a-Cath requires specially trained healthcare professionals to access the implanted reservoir using a Huber needle for chemotherapy administration.

### Clinical Findings
Through clinical observations and stakeholder interviews, our team sought to better understand the challenges associated with existing vascular access methods for chemotherapy administration. A total of 31 stakeholder interviews were conducted, comprising 8 interventional radiology (IR) clinicians and staff, 4 medical oncology clinicians and staff, 3 BD industry personnel, and 16 patients.

Overall, the findings indicated that the Port-a-Cath, the current gold standard, remains a well-established and effective solution for long-term chemotherapy administration, with generally positive feedback from both healthcare professionals and patients. Most stakeholders expressed satisfaction with its reliability, functionality, and suitability for repeated chemotherapy administration.

However, one significant finding emerged from our interviews with medical oncology clinicians: approximately 50% of patients who were eligible for a Port-a-Cath reportedly declined implantation, despite its clinical benefits. Common reasons cited included an aversion to undergoing an additional invasive procedure, reluctance to have a foreign object permanently implanted in the body, and concerns that the port might interfere with their daily activities.

For patients who decline port implantation, chemotherapy may instead be administered through PIVC. However, repeated peripheral administration of chemotherapy can cause venous irritation and inflammation, potentially resulting in phlebitis in approximately 30% of patients (Sudhakar et al., 2023). As peripheral veins become increasingly inflamed, scarred, or unsuitable for cannulation, establishing reliable venous access may become progressively more challenging. This can lead to repeated cannulation attempts, increased pain and discomfort, and potential delays in chemotherapy administration. Inevitably, some patients may eventually require a PICC or Port-a-Cath to continue treatment safely and reliably, despite their initial reluctance to undergo central venous access placement.

#### Refinement of the Clinical Need Statement
Based on these clinical findings, our team recognised that the primary challenge was not necessarily to improve the existing Port-a-Cath, but rather to address the unmet needs of patients who are unwilling to undergo port implantation. While Port-a-Caths remain an effective solution for long-term chemotherapy administration, a significant proportion of eligible patients may decline them due to concerns about procedural invasiveness, the presence of an implanted foreign body, and potential interference with daily activities.

This insight prompted our team to shift our focus from redesigning existing central venous access devices towards exploring ways to make peripheral intravenous access safer and more sustainable for patients undergoing repeated chemotherapy cycles. In particular, we identified chemotherapy-induced phlebitis and progressive peripheral venous damage as key challenges that could compromise vascular access over the course of treatment.

As such, our team revised the initial problem statement to address this unmet clinical need: **There is a need for a way to protect peripheral veins from chemotherapy-induced damage in port-eligible patients who decline a port, reducing chemotherapy-related phlebitis from 30% to below 15%, so that peripheral access stays usable for the full course of treatment.**

### Value Proposition
Building upon the identified market gap, our team developed the following value proposition for Lifeline: **For cancer patients requiring repeated intravenous chemotherapy who decline port implantation, Lifeline aims to protect peripheral veins from chemotherapy-induced damage without the need for surgery or external tubing, enabling patients to complete their treatment with greater comfort and fewer vascular access-related complications.**

Ultimately, Lifeline seeks to bridge the gap between existing peripheral and central venous access solutions by addressing both the clinical need for reliable chemotherapy administration and patients' preferences for less invasive treatment options.

## Design Phase
### Design Criteria
Before developing potential solutions, our team established a set of must-have and good-to-have design criteria based on the clinical needs and stakeholder insights identified during the discovery phase. These criteria served as a framework for evaluating potential concepts, ensuring that the proposed solutions would address key clinical challenges while remaining practical for implementation.

As illustrated in Figure 3, the must-have criteria represent the essential requirements that every proposed concept should fulfil. These include maintaining an infection rate of 2% or lower, using the estimated infection rate of existing Port-a-Caths as a benchmark, ensuring compatibility with chemotherapy drugs, including vesicants, and preserving peripheral vein patency throughout a minimum treatment duration of three months. Additionally, device insertion and removal should be less invasive than Port-a-Cath implantation. The good-to-have criteria focus on enhancing clinical usability, versatility, and patient convenience. These include compatibility with existing clinical workflows and other intravenous therapies, ease of use, and minimal disruption to patients' daily activities.

### Concept Generation
Guided by these design criteria, our team explored a range of potential solutions aimed at reducing chemotherapy-induced peripheral venous damage. As shown in Figure 4, we broadly categorised the concepts into physical protection, chemical protection, and alternative vascular access designs.

Physical protection concepts focused on modifying catheter designs or fluid flow patterns to minimise direct contact between chemotherapy drugs and the venous wall, while chemical protection concepts explored the use of protective fluids or agents to reduce venous irritation and inflammation. Alternative vascular access designs were also considered to address limitations associated with existing devices. Following preliminary discussions and evaluation, five concepts were shortlisted for further assessment: telescoping catheter, wearable flat port, multi-outlet catheter, coaxial liquid flow, and chemical vein protection.

### Concept Evaluation and Selection
The five shortlisted concepts were evaluated against the established must-have and good-to-have criteria using a scoring matrix, with each criterion rated on a scale of 1 to 5, where 5 represented the highest level of suitability, as illustrated in Figure 5.

The telescoping catheter received a score of 30/45, primarily due to its procedural complexity. As the concept involves advancing a catheter further into the vasculature during each treatment session, similar to aspects of PICC insertion, it may require specially trained personnel, making it less compatible with existing clinical workflows. The wearable flat port received the lowest score of 25/45, largely due to concerns regarding infection risk associated with its externally accessible components.

The chemical vein protection concept received a score of 31/45, with technical feasibility being its main limitation. Identifying a suitable protective agent would require careful consideration of its anti-inflammatory properties, chemical compatibility with chemotherapy drugs, and potential effects on drug stability, therapeutic efficacy, and patient safety.

The multi-outlet catheter and coaxial liquid flow concepts achieved the highest overall scores of 40/45 each. Both concepts demonstrated potential for reducing direct chemotherapy exposure to the venous wall while maintaining a relatively minimally invasive approach. To further assess their technical feasibility, our team reviewed existing literature investigating catheter design modifications and controlled fluid flow patterns.

A study demonstrated that modifying standard peripheral IV angiocatheters by adding side holes/slits resulted in a 9–30% reduction in the velocity of the exiting jet, making it less well-defined, qualitatively altering the overall flow field, and reducing wall shear stress (Weber et al., 2009).

Similarly, another study demonstrated the application of core-annular flow to improve the injectability of highly concentrated drug formulations. This approach utilises a coaxial flow arrangement in which the drug formulation is concentrated within a central fluid core, surrounded by an outer lubricating fluid layer (Jayaprakash et al., 2020). Although the study focused on improving drug injectability rather than preventing chemotherapy-induced phlebitis, it provides a useful foundation for investigating whether a similar flow configuration could establish a protective fluid layer between chemotherapy drugs and the venous wall.

These findings provided preliminary technical support for both concepts, demonstrating how modifications to catheter geometry and fluid flow arrangements could potentially influence drug delivery patterns. Although both concepts performed equally well in the initial evaluation, our team ultimately selected coaxial liquid flow for the prototyping phase. This approach offers the potential to establish a surrounding fluid layer that minimises direct contact between chemotherapy drugs and the venous wall. The subsequent prototyping phase will therefore focus on investigating the feasibility of generating and maintaining a stable coaxial flow configuration under conditions relevant to peripheral intravenous infusion.

## Prototyping Phase
### Concept design: Coaxial Flow Catheter
Following the concept evaluation, our team developed a preliminary coaxial flow catheter design aimed at minimising direct contact between chemotherapy drugs and the peripheral venous wall. As illustrated in Figure 6, the proposed catheter incorporates a dual-flow arrangement, consisting of a central channel for chemotherapy delivery and a surrounding channel for saline or other potentially suitable secondary fluids.

<img width="1280" height="720" alt="Run 5 - DHIP Concept Presentation Template pptx (3)" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/1ca00770-5a93-45d0-ac2d-31845204bc2a" />
<p align="center">
  <strong>Figure 6:</strong> <em>Coaxial Flow Catheter Design</em>
</p>

The design aims to establish a core-annular flow configuration, where chemotherapy is delivered through the central channel while a secondary fluid flows through the surrounding channel, forming a protective fluid layer between the chemotherapy stream and the venous wall. By maintaining the chemotherapy stream near the centre of the vessel, the surrounding fluid could potentially reduce direct exposure of the venous endothelium to chemotherapy drugs, thereby minimising irritation and inflammation. For the initial design, saline was selected as the secondary fluid. However, alternative fluids may also be explored, subject to further testing and optimisation to evaluate their suitability, chemical compatibility with chemotherapy drugs, and potential effects on drug stability, therapeutic efficacy, and patient safety. 

The effectiveness of this approach would depend on several factors, including the relative flow rates of chemotherapy and the secondary fluid, catheter geometry, fluid properties, and interactions with the surrounding blood flow. These parameters will be investigated during the prototyping phase to determine whether a stable coaxial flow arrangement can be established and maintained under conditions representative of peripheral intravenous infusion.

### Prototyping Findings

#### CFD Methodology and Evaluation Criteria
To evaluate the technical feasibility of the proposed catheter design, our team developed three-dimensional computer-aided design (CAD) models and conducted computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations using ANSYS Fluent. The simulations investigated whether modifications to catheter outlet geometry and infusion flow patterns could reduce chemotherapy exposure and mechanical stress at the peripheral venous wall.

Three catheter configurations were evaluated: a conventional peripheral intravenous catheter (PIVC) serving as the baseline, a six-hole catheter incorporating three pairs of side openings, and a dual-lumen catheter delivering chemotherapy through a central lumen surrounded by an annular saline flow.

To facilitate comparison, all three models used a 4 mm diameter, 100 mm long vein, with a centrally positioned catheter extending 30 mm into the vessel. The revised catheter models had an outer diameter of 1.4 mm. The conventional and six-hole catheters delivered a premixed chemotherapy–saline solution, whereas the dual-lumen catheter delivered the two fluids through separate concentric passages.

Chemotherapy and saline flow rates were initially fixed at 1 mL/min each, while the blood inlet velocity was maintained at 0.05 m/s. A simplified three-species transport model was implemented to represent blood, saline and chemotherapy solution, with chemotherapy mass fraction serving as a proxy for chemical exposure.

Performance was evaluated using three categories of metrics:
1. Chemical exposure: Peak and area-weighted mean chemotherapy mass fraction at the vein wall.
2. Mechanical stress: Peak and area-weighted mean wall shear stress (WSS).
3. Flow characteristics: Distal tip velocity, velocity contours, flow pathlines and downstream tracer distribution.

The initial simulations compared the conventional PIVC, six-hole PIVC and dual-lumen catheter at a 1:1 chemotherapy-to-saline flow-rate ratio.

The six-hole catheter demonstrated limited improvement over the conventional baseline. Although the additional openings were intended to redistribute the infused solution and reduce localized mechanical stress, the resulting mean WSS remained nearly unchanged, decreasing by only 0.07%. Meanwhile, peak and mean vein-wall chemotherapy mass fractions increased by approximately 5.2% and 0.7%, respectively.

These findings suggest that introducing additional side openings does not necessarily reduce chemical exposure at the vein wall. The effectiveness of multi-hole designs may depend on factors such as hole placement, orientation and the distribution of flow between the distal and lateral openings.

In contrast, the dual-lumen catheter demonstrated a substantial reduction in predicted vein-wall chemotherapy exposure. Compared with the conventional baseline, peak and mean wall chemotherapy mass fractions decreased by approximately 81.1% and 89.8%, respectively.

This finding supports the proposed mechanism of using an annular saline stream to limit chemotherapy transport towards the venous wall. Rather than premixing chemotherapy and saline before infusion, the dual-lumen configuration introduces saline around the central chemotherapy stream, potentially promoting a lower-concentration region near the vein wall.

However, this improvement was accompanied by increased mechanical stress. Peak WSS increased by approximately 18.5%, while mean WSS increased by approximately 16.4% compared with the baseline. This indicates a potential trade-off between reducing chemical exposure and increasing mechanical loading on the venous wall.

Overall, the initial comparison identified the dual-lumen configuration as the more promising design for reducing predicted chemotherapy exposure, although further optimization is required to address the associated increase in WSS.

#### Flow-Ratio Sensitivity Study

Following the initial design comparison, a parametric CFD study was conducted to investigate how the relative chemotherapy and saline flow rates influence the performance of the dual-lumen catheter.

The chemotherapy flow rate was maintained at 1 mL/min, while the saline flow rate was varied between 0.5, 1 and 2 mL/min, corresponding to chemotherapy-to-saline flow-rate ratios of 1:0.5, 1:1 and 1:2.

[Insert Table 2: Dual-lumen CFD results across different chemotherapy-to-saline flow-rate ratios]

Increasing the saline flow rate produced a consistent reduction in chemotherapy tracer exposure at the vein wall. As the saline flow increased from 0.5 to 2 mL/min, peak wall tracer mass fraction decreased from 0.002034 to 0.001908, representing a reduction of approximately 6.2%. Similarly, mean wall tracer mass fraction decreased from 0.0003253 to 0.0002975, corresponding to an 8.5% reduction.

These findings suggest that increasing annular saline flow may improve the confinement of chemotherapy within the central bloodstream, reducing its transport towards the venous wall.

However, mean WSS increased slightly from 0.4768 Pa to 0.4858 Pa, representing an increase of approximately 1.9%. This suggests that greater saline flow may improve chemical shielding at the expense of slightly increased mechanical stress.

Among the three conditions tested, the 1:2 chemotherapy-to-saline ratio produced the lowest predicted vein-wall chemotherapy exposure. Nevertheless, this condition also required the greatest saline volume, and its suitability must be evaluated against fluid administration requirements and potential clinical constraints.

These results demonstrate that the protective performance of the dual-lumen design is sensitive to the saline flow rate, providing an opportunity for further optimization of the balance between chemical exposure, mechanical stress and fluid delivery.

#### Initial Outcomes and Technical Feasibility

The preliminary CFD results provide encouraging evidence of the technical potential of the proposed coaxial flow catheter.

Most significantly, the dual-lumen configuration achieved an approximately 90% reduction in mean vein-wall chemotherapy tracer mass fraction relative to the conventional premixed-infusion baseline under matched total infusion conditions. This demonstrates that modifying the spatial arrangement of chemotherapy and saline delivery can substantially influence predicted chemical exposure at the venous wall.

Furthermore, the flow-ratio sensitivity study demonstrated a consistent reduction in wall tracer exposure with increasing saline flow, indicating that the performance of the proposed design can be influenced through adjustable operating parameters.

Beyond the numerical findings, the successful development of the CAD models, generation of computational meshes, implementation of multi-species fluid transport and extraction of quantitative performance metrics establishes a functional computational prototyping framework. This framework enables systematic evaluation and refinement of catheter geometry and infusion conditions before physical fabrication.

Collectively, these outcomes demonstrate promising progress towards the project's intended deliverables, particularly the development of a minimally invasive catheter concept capable of reducing predicted chemotherapy exposure at the peripheral vein wall.

However, these results establish preliminary computational feasibility rather than clinical effectiveness. The observed increase in WSS, together with the need for experimental verification, indicates that further development is necessary before the proposed catheter can be considered suitable for clinical application.

#### Limitations and Future Development

Several limitations must be addressed in subsequent development stages.

First, the simulations assumed a straight, rigid vein with a centrally positioned catheter and steady, laminar flow. Actual peripheral veins exhibit anatomical variability, vessel compliance and differences in catheter positioning, which may affect the resulting flow patterns.

Second, chemotherapy was represented using a simplified species transport model rather than a specific drug formulation. Consequently, the predicted tracer distribution does not directly quantify endothelial toxicity, phlebitis risk or long-term vascular damage.

Third, the flow-ratio study varied the total saline infusion volume. Therefore, the observed improvements may reflect a combination of hydrodynamic shielding, additional dilution and changes in the local flow field.

Future work will focus on refining the dual-lumen geometry and saline flow conditions, conducting mesh-independence and model-validation studies, and developing physical flow models to experimentally investigate the predicted tracer distribution. Subsequent biological testing would be required to determine whether reduced chemotherapy exposure translates into reduced endothelial injury.

These investigations will guide the development of a clinically feasible solution that balances chemical protection, mechanical safety and practical compatibility with chemotherapy administration.

## Conclusion
Through clinical observations, stakeholder interviews, and market analysis, our team identified an unmet need among cancer patients who decline Port-a-Cath implantation despite requiring repeated chemotherapy administration. This led us to shift our focus towards developing a minimally invasive solution that protects peripheral veins from chemotherapy-induced damage while preserving reliable venous access throughout treatment. Following concept generation, evaluation, and preliminary literature research, coaxial liquid flow was selected as the most promising concept for further development. By exploring the use of a protective fluid layer between chemotherapy drugs and the venous wall, our team aims to reduce the incidence of chemotherapy-related phlebitis and improve the overall treatment experience for patients.

Moving forward, our team will continue prototype development and testing to optimise key flow parameters and evaluate additional catheter designs, with the aim of developing a final solution that can effectively minimise chemotherapy exposure to the venous wall. We will also continue engaging with medical oncology clinicians and nurses to better understand chemotherapy drug interactions, compatibility considerations, and practical requirements for clinical implementation. Additionally, patient feedback will be gathered to evaluate the proposed solution against existing vascular access methods, particularly in terms of comfort, convenience, and willingness to adopt the device.
